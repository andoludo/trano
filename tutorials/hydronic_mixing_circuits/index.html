    Hydronic heating with mixing circuits

    This tutorial heats the three zones of the hydronic heating tutorial with a hydronic system wired the way a heating engineer would wire it: each group of radiators has a mixing circuit with its pump after its three-way valve. The boiler can then run hot while the radiators get the water temperature they are designed for.

    Why the pump goes after the three-way valve

    A three-way valve controlled on temperature has to mix: it blends the hot water of the boiler (port_1) with the cooler water coming back from the radiators (port_3) and sends the blend to the radiators (port_2). Its controller measures the temperature after the valve and opens or closes the valve to hold it at a set point.

    Mixing only happens if something pulls the return water into port_3: the pump of the circuit, placed after the valve.

    boiler ──► port_1 ┐
                  ├ THREE-WAY VALVE ── port_2 ──► sensor ──► pump ──► radiators ──► radiator valves
          port_3 ─┘                                                                       │
            ▲                                                                             ▼
            └──────────────────────────────── split valve ◄───────────────────────────────┘
                                                   │
                                                   └──► back to the boiler

    When the pump is placed before the valve, as in the earlier hydronic tutorials, it pushes the water into port_1: the water then leaves through port_3 back to the boiler instead of being drawn in. The valve no longer mixes, it diverts: closing it sends hot water back to the boiler instead of to the radiators, but the water that does reach the radiators is still as hot as the boiler supply. The valve can cut the flow, never lower the temperature. If the boiler supply exceeds the set point of the valve, the controller closes the valve completely and the rooms go cold: with the default boiler set point of 80 degC, the top of the storage tank reaches about 95 degC, above the default 90 degC of the valve, and the rooms of such a model fell to 16 degC. Trano reports that layout with a HydronicLayoutWarning (see the note in the hydronic heating tutorial).

    Configuration of the systems

    The envelope, the occupancy and the emissions are those of the hydronic heating tutorial; only the systems change. Each mixing circuit is a chain three_way_valve -> temperature_sensor -> pump -> radiators:

    • the boiler feeds the three-way valves directly (outlets of the boiler);
    • the three-way valve lists the split valve of its circuit in its inlets: that is the return water mixed in through port_3. Its control holds supply_temperature_setpoint (70 degC here; 90 degC when absent) with a PI controller (see below);
    • the temperature sensor comes right after the valve: its control measures the mixed water there;
    • the pump comes after the sensor and feeds the radiators of the circuit. Each circuit has its own pump; a single pump before several valves cannot make them mix;
    • the split valve collects the return of the radiator valves and sends it to the boiler; the three-way valve draws its share of it.

    Tuning the valve controller

    three_way_valve_control takes the parameters of its controller (controller_type, k, ti, ...; see the parameters). This example uses a PI controller with a gain of 0.1 per kelvin and an integral time of 300 s; the lessons learned at the end of this page explain why.

    The model is created as in the other tutorials:

    Test three zones hydronic mixing
    def test_three_zones_hydronic_mixing() -> None:
    from trano.main import create_model

    create_model(
        path_to_yaml_configuration_folder / "three_zones_hydronic_mixing.yaml",
        library="IDEAS",
    )

    Results

    Two January weeks simulated with IDEAS. The rooms are heated to 21 degC from 7 to 19 h and fall back to 16 degC at night; the boiler, with its default 80 degC set point, charges its storage tank up to about 95 degC.

    The figure below shows the mixing at work. While the top of the tank is above 75 degC (it rises to 94 degC), the water sent to the radiators stays at the 70 degC set point of the valves (median 70.0 degC; 95 % of the time below 76 degC; it dips when the rooms start heating after the night setback and the valves open fully). The rooms average 20.3 to 20.5 degC during the occupied hours. With the pump before the valves, that water would follow the tank temperature.

    Lessons learned: the PID of the three-way valves

    These lessons come from simulating three January days of this model with IDEAS, changing only the controller of the two valves. The figures are for the water sent to the radiators while the top of the tank is above 75 degC.

    Controller of the valves Water to the radiators: median 95 % of the time below Rooms (occupied, mean) Time to simulate
    Default: proportional, k = 1 per K 68 to 69 degC 75 degC 20.3 to 20.4 degC 451 s
    Proportional, k = 0.1 per K 63 degC 71 degC 20.2 to 20.3 degC 128 s
    PI, k = 0.1 per K, ti = 300 s 70.0 degC 76 degC 20.3 to 20.4 degC 110 s
    Pump before the valves (earlier tutorial), default controller follows the tank — — 25 s

    1. The tuning only matters once the valve really regulates. With the pump before the valves, the valves sit fully open, the controller never acts, and the default tuning looks fine: that model simulates in 25 s. The same controller on valves that do mix made the simulation 18 times slower. A layout that works hides the weaknesses of its controls, so a model only tells you about its controller once the controller has something to do.

    2. The gain has units, and 1 per kelvin is a lot for a valve. The controller turns a temperature error in kelvin into a valve opening between 0 and 1. With k = 1, an error of 1 K already moves the valve over its whole stroke. Between the valve and the sensor there are delays: the mixing volume, the sensor's time constant, the travel of the water and, in IDEAS, the actuator of the valve. A large gain acting through delays overshoots, corrects and overshoots again: the valve hunts around the set point, and every reversal is a fast change the solver follows with small steps. We did not record the valve finely enough to see each reversal, but the timings point to it: lowering the gain alone cut the simulation from 451 s to 128 s.

    3. A proportional controller cannot reach its set point. It only opens the valve in proportion to the error, so it needs a permanent error to hold the valve open: the opening divided by k. With k = 1 and the valves about 70 % open, that offset is 0.7 K (68 to 69 degC for a 70 degC set point), which is why it goes unnoticed. Lowering the gain to calm the valve makes the offset ten times larger: k = 0.1 held the water at 63 degC, 7 K below the set point. A proportional controller makes you choose between a calm valve and an accurate temperature.

    4. The integral action removes that choice. A PI controller keeps adding up the error, so it holds the set point with a small gain: 70.0 degC with k = 0.1, in 110 s. Its integral time must be slow compared with the delays of the circuit (mixing volume, sensor, valve actuator): 300 s here.

    5. Never switch to PI without setting ti. The default ti is 0.5 s. It is unused by the proportional controller, but it becomes active as soon as controller_type is set to PI. An integral time of half a second is far faster than the delays of the circuit, a classic cause of oscillation (we did not simulate it). Always give controller_type, k and ti together.

    6. How to check a tuning. Plot the valve opening and the temperature after the valve (as in the second figure of the results):

    • the opening zig-zags, or the simulation is unusually slow: the gain is too high, lower k;
    • the temperature settles away from the set point: there is no integral action, use PI;
    • the temperature drifts slowly back and forth around the set point: the integral is too fast, raise ti.

    The PI values of this example are a starting point that worked here. We tested only the three tunings above, so check them on your own circuit with the plots above rather than reusing them blindly. The default of three_way_valve_control is still the proportional controller, so that the existing models keep their results.
